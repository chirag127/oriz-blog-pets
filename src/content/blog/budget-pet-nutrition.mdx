Good pet nutrition and a tight budget are not enemies. Most of what pet marketing sells is packaging, buzzwords, and fear. The parts that actually keep an animal healthy are cheaper and simpler than the premium shelf suggests. Here is how to feed a dog or cat well without wasting money.

## Learn the one label that matters

In many markets, pet food carries a nutritional adequacy statement — the line that says the food is "complete and balanced" for a given life stage (growth, maintenance, or all life stages), ideally backed by a recognised standard such as AAFCO.

That single line matters more than any front-of-bag adjective. "Premium," "holistic," "gourmet," and "natural" are **marketing terms with no legal definition.** A modestly priced food that is certified complete and balanced beats an expensive one that is not.

Two practical rules:

- **Match the life stage.** Puppies and kittens need growth formulas; adults need maintenance. Feeding kitten food to a sedentary adult cat is a fast route to obesity.
- **Cats are obligate carnivores.** They require animal-based protein and nutrients like taurine that they cannot make themselves. Never feed a cat a dog food or a vegetarian diet — it can cause serious, sometimes fatal, deficiencies.

## Where to spend, where to save

Spend on:

- **A complete-and-balanced food appropriate to the life stage.** This is the non-negotiable.
- **Correct portions** (more on this below) — the cheapest health intervention there is.

Save on:

- **Fancy branding and boutique "trend" diets.** Grain-free, raw, and exotic-protein diets are often marketed as premium but are not automatically healthier; some carry real risks and should only be fed on veterinary advice.
- **Most supplements.** A healthy pet on a complete diet rarely needs add-ons. Do not spend on supplements unless a vet recommends them for a specific reason.
- **Excess treats.** Treats should be **no more than about 10% of daily calories.** Beyond that you are paying to unbalance the diet you carefully chose.

## Portion control is free and powerful

Overfeeding is the most expensive habit in pet ownership, because obesity drives arthritis, diabetes, and a shorter life — all of which cost far more than the extra food.

- **Measure meals with a cup or a kitchen scale**, not by eye. "Eyeballing it" almost always drifts upward.
- **Use the bag's guidelines as a starting point, then adjust to the animal**, not the reverse. Feeding charts are generous and assume an active adult.
- **Learn to score body condition.** You should be able to feel the ribs without pressing hard, and see a visible waist from above and a tuck from the side. If not, cut portions by 10% and reassess in a few weeks.

Portion control costs nothing and quietly saves you the biggest vet bills of a pet's life.

## Buy smart, store smart

- **Buy the right-sized bag for how fast you use it.** Dry food goes stale and rancid once opened; a giant bag for one small cat is false economy.
- **Store dry food in an airtight container**, ideally keeping it in the original bag inside the bin — the bag has a barrier layer and the batch code you would need for a recall.
- **Watch unit price, not sticker price.** Compare cost per kilo across sizes and brands. Larger bags of a food you go through quickly are usually cheaper per serving.
- **Transition foods gradually** over about a week, mixing increasing amounts of the new food into the old. A sudden switch to save a few rupees often buys you a bout of diarrhoea instead.

## Fresh water and free prevention

Two more things that cost almost nothing and matter a lot:

- **Constant access to clean, fresh water.** Cats in particular are prone to under-drinking, which stresses the urinary tract. Many do better with a wider bowl or a pet fountain, and wet food adds moisture to the diet.
- **Prevention beats treatment, always.** Steady portions, a suitable complete diet, and keeping weight in check prevent conditions that are miserable for the pet and brutal for your wallet.

Feed a life-stage-appropriate, complete-and-balanced food in measured portions, keep treats to a tenth of the day, and give constant fresh water. Do that and you are giving your dog or cat genuinely good nutrition — while spending less than the person one aisle over who bought the bag with the prettiest label.
