Bringing a puppy home is thrilling and, by about hour six, quietly terrifying. The good news: puppies do not need you to be perfect. They need you to be predictable. This is the plan I give friends — a boring, repeatable structure that turns the first week from chaos into a routine your puppy can actually learn.

## Before the puppy arrives

Set the environment up so good behaviour is the easy default. You want fewer decisions to make while sleep-deprived.

- **A crate sized to stand, turn, and lie down** — no bigger. A too-large crate lets a puppy toilet in one corner and sleep in another, which sabotages house-training.
- **A confinement zone** (a playpen or a puppy-proofed kitchen) so the puppy is never loose and unsupervised.
- **Cheap enzymatic cleaner**, not a regular one. Normal cleaners leave scent markers a dog's nose reads as "toilet here."
- **The same food the breeder or shelter was feeding.** Switch foods gradually over a week; an abrupt change plus stress equals diarrhoea on night one.

## Day 1: arrival and the first night

Keep day one deliberately dull. Skip the party. A new puppy has just left its mother and littermates, and a house full of excited visitors is overwhelming.

Carry the puppy to your chosen toilet spot the moment you get home, wait, and reward calmly if it goes. Then let it explore one room on a long lead.

The first night is the hard one. Put the crate **next to your bed**. A puppy that can hear and smell you settles faster than one shut in a distant kitchen crying alone. Expect one or two wake-ups to toilet — take the puppy out on-lead, no play, no talking, back to the crate. You are teaching that night-time is boring.

## Days 2 to 4: build the schedule

Puppies thrive on rhythm. Toilet trips go out **after every sleep, every meal, every play session, and roughly every hour otherwise.** Yes, that is a lot. It is also the whole game — most house-training failures are supervision failures, not the dog's fault.

A simple loop that works:

1. Wake / arrive → straight outside.
2. Reward the instant they finish (not when they come back inside — timing is everything).
3. Ten to twenty minutes of play or gentle training.
4. Water, then a settle in the crate or pen for a nap.

Puppies sleep **16 to 20 hours a day.** An overtired puppy behaves like an overtired toddler — bitey, frantic, unable to settle. If yours has gone feral, the answer is usually a nap, not more stimulation.

## Days 5 to 7: names, handling, and the vet

Start short, happy training sessions — two minutes, a few times a day. Teach a name response and a "sit" using food they love. Keep it fun and quit while they still want more.

Handle your puppy daily: touch paws, ears, gums, and tail, pairing each with a treat. You are banking cooperation for future nail trims and vet exams.

Book a **vet check in the first few days.** Confirm the vaccination and deworming schedule, discuss parasite prevention, and ask about microchipping if it is not done. Get pet insurance sorted now, before any condition can be called pre-existing.

## The socialisation window closes fast

This is the part people miss. A puppy's key socialisation window runs to roughly **16 weeks of age.** Positive, gentle exposure during this window shapes how confident and stable the adult dog becomes.

Before full vaccination you cannot put a puppy on the ground in public spaces, but you are not stuck:

- Carry them to see traffic, crowds, and other environments from your arms.
- Invite calm, vaccinated adult dogs to your home.
- Let them meet people of different ages, hear the vacuum and doorbell, and ride in the car.

Reward calm curiosity; never force a frightened puppy toward something scary. One bad flooding experience can create a lasting fear.

## What to expect emotionally

Around day three or four many new owners hit the "puppy blues" — exhaustion, doubt, wondering what they have done. It is extremely common and it passes. You are not failing. You are running on broken sleep while raising a small animal that bites and does not use a toilet yet.

Give it two weeks. By then the schedule is muscle memory, the night wakings are shrinking, and you will catch your puppy doing something so ridiculous that the whole exhausting week suddenly feels worth it.
